## Observations

I used only matrix algebra on the supplied block weights—no forward passes. **The strongest evidence is for ordered text with local-context and copying circuits. The corpus identification is considerably less certain.**

For the numerical evidence below, define:
- \(C=I-\mathbf1\mathbf1^\top/384\), \(G_l=\operatorname{diag}(\texttt{ln1_w.l})C\).
- \(A_{lh}=G_l^\top W_{q,lh}^\top W_{k,lh}G_l/8\): a weight-derived QK matrix.
- \(B_{lh}=CW_{o,lh}W_{v,lh}G_l\): a weight-derived OV matrix.

These are algebraic diagnostics, not evaluated attention patterns.

1. **Two early heads appear to transport character information using a different addressing subspace.**  
   From the leading 28 right singular vectors of stacked \(B_{01},B_{05}\), I obtained a candidate character-information subspace. It captures **93.1% and 91.2%** of those two OV matrices’ squared Frobenius norms, respectively, but only **6.7–12.5%** of their QK query/key energy. This separation is consistent with positional addressing of character-valued information. L0H5 is my strongest previous-character candidate; L0H1 likely accesses another nearby offset.

2. **L0H4 is an unusually weak-addressing, strong-writing head.**  
   Its QK Frobenius norm is approximately **0.01**, versus **0.88** for L0H1 and **0.97** for L0H5. Its centered OV singular values start **13.877, 9.644, 2.507**. This suggests broad aggregation into a small number of contextual or positional features.

3. **Layer 1 repeatedly reads those shared features.**  
   Let \(P\) contain the first two left singular vectors of \(B_{04}\). For L1H1–L1H5, **47.9–58.2%** of QK energy lies on that two-dimensional key-side subspace. It also captures **82.4–93.0%** of their affine key-preference vectors, derived from `ln1_b.1`, `W_q.1`, and `W_k.1`. This supports a common addressing mechanism, plausibly recency-related, rather than six independent semantic retrieval mechanisms.

4. **Several later heads have a clear induction-compatible composition.**  
   Let \(T\) be the leading 24-dimensional candidate character subspace above, and define
   \[
   I_h=\frac{\operatorname{tr}(T^\top A_hB_{05}T)}
   {\|T^\top A_hB_{05}T\|_F}.
   \]
   The values are **2.85 for L2H1, 2.72 for L3H1, and 2.89 for L3H5**. Their compressed products have respectively **24, 22, and 22** eigenvalues with positive real parts, out of 24. The positive alignment persists across subspace ranks 16–64. If L0H5 supplies previous-character information, these are precisely the sorts of key-side matching compositions expected in induction circuits.

**Limits:** The missing embeddings prevent labeling these directions as particular characters or positions. Consequently, “previous,” “local,” and “copy” below are hypotheses—not measured attention behavior.

## Reasoning

**As-is text fits substantially better than shuffled text.** The combination of early positional character transport and later positive key-side compositions suggests exploiting ordered sequences and repeated continuations. Line-shuffled text retains useful character counts and line structure, but greatly weakens the usefulness of that particular combination.

**Python is my best, moderately uncertain corpus guess.** Repeated identifiers and boilerplate make contextual copying especially valuable, while line structure provides useful shared addressing features. English remains a substantial alternative: these mechanisms are not code-specific. I put romaji third, but cannot identify its absence from unlabeled weight directions alone.

Thus, my overall interpretation is: **nearby-character feature construction, a low-dimensional shared context/addressing mechanism, and several later repetition-retrieval circuits.** Exact attention distances remain unverified.
